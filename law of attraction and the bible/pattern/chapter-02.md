Chapter 1 set out ten principles, and the first one was like attracts like. Energy attracts similar energy. Your dominant thoughts, emotions, and beliefs act like a magnet, drawing people, events, and circumstances that match your internal state.

That was the claim in the words people use for it now. What Chapter 1 never asked is where it came from.

It is worth asking, because if there is anything to it, it cannot be new. Something that basic about being alive would have been noticed long before anyone thought to hold a seminar about it. Somebody would have written it down. Probably several somebodies, in places with no knowledge of each other, in whatever words they had.

That is more or less what happened, and the trail runs through stranger country than you would expect. A scholar with the ear of a Chinese emperor. A book of divination older than most nations. A circle of mystics in medieval Spain. And a camp full of frightened people on the edge of a land they never entered.

Start with the camp. It is the hardest of the four, and it is the one the rest of the chapter has to answer for.

The story is in Numbers, the fourth book of the Hebrew Bible, which follows Israel through the wilderness years after the escape from Egypt.  Chapters 13 and 14 tell how Israel sent twelve men to scout Canaan, and what happened when they brought their report back to the camp at Kadesh, on the southern edge of the land they had been promised.

Twelve men had walked north into Canaan to look at the land, and they came back carrying proof that it was good. One cluster of grapes was heavy enough that two of them slung it on a pole between their shoulders. They brought pomegranates. They brought figs. They set the fruit down in front of the whole assembly at Kadesh, and then ten of the twelve started talking about the walls.

The cities were fortified. The people were tall. There were giants in that land, and next to them, one of the scouts said, we looked like grasshoppers. Two men argued back. Caleb said they could go up and take it. Joshua said the same. Nobody listened to either of them, because by then the camp had heard the word giants and could not hear anything else. They were not only talking. In later language that would be visualization — imagination fixed on giants and graves until that was all they could see.

What happened that night is the part worth focusing on. The people wept, and they wept all night, and while they wept they talked. What they said had a shape to it, and the writer of Numbers records it in the first four verses of chapter 14. Better if we had died in Egypt. Better if we had died out here in this desert. God has brought us all this way to be killed by the sword, and our wives and our children will be carried off as spoil. Then someone said the thing that turns a bad night into a decision. Let us choose a leader and go back.

They said it to each other, out loud, over and over, until the saying of it was the only thing left in the camp. In modern language, that would be called an affirmation or spoken words held until they become belief. At Kadesh they affirmed defeat.

And this is what they were told, in Numbers 14:28. As I live, says the LORD, what you have said in my hearing, I will do to you. The next verses spell out what that means. You said you would die in this wilderness, so you will die in this wilderness. You said your children would be carried off, and your children are the ones I will bring into the land. 

Not one detail of that answer was invented. Every piece of it was handed back to them in the words they had used. They spent a night describing a future, and the future they received was the one they described. Modern teaching would call that a manifestation.  Only this one ran the wrong way.

That story is old and it is hard, and it does not read like a lesson in positive thinking. It reads like something stranger. It reads like a people discovering that what they had been rehearsing all night was not private, was not weightless, and was not going to burn off at sunrise.

Which raises the question. Why would anything work that way?

The modern answer is that the universe does not care whether an affirmation is preceived to be good or bad. It does not sort speech into right and wrong, but simply reacts to the vibration that is offered. At Kadesh, the camp offered fear, defeat, and a return to Egypt. That was the vibration. That was what came back.  Chapter 1 puts the same answer in other words. Like attracts like, energy attracts similar energy, and a dominant inner state draws a matching outer result. Vibration and frequency are newer labels for that claim, but those concepts belong to books written centuries ago.

The camp at Kadesh had never read any of those books. Neither had the men in the next few pages. They lived in different centuries, on different continents, in traditions that had no contact with one another. What they share is that each of them watched ordinary life for a long time and came away convinced of the same strange thing. What a person holds inside does not stay inside. It goes out looking for its own kind.

## A man in the Han court

Around a hundred and fifty years before Christ, a Confucian scholar named Dong Zhongshu had the ear of a Chinese emperor, and he used it to make an argument that sounds superstitious until you look at what he was actually claiming. When government went bad, he said, the natural world answered with drought, flood, and strange weather. Heaven was not lashing out at random. Heaven was responding the way one thing responds to another thing of its own kind. The Chinese name for that responding is *ganying*, and Dong Zhongshu is the man most associated with it.

Ganying, literally "stimulus and response" or "sympathetic resonance", is the foundational ancient Chinese concept that all things in the universe belong to interrelated categories and naturally resonate with one another when they share the same cosmic frequency.

Rather than viewing cause-and-effect as a mechanical collision (like one billiard ball hitting another), ganying views the cosmos as an interconnected organism. When an action, emotion, or event occurs in one part of the system, it automatically pulls, triggers, or vibrates a corresponding response in things of the same kind across space and time—much like striking one string on a tuned lute causes the matching string on a nearby instrument to hum.

The book that carries his name is the *Chunqiu fanlu* which translates into English as something like *Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals*.  The *Chunqiu fanlu* is a collection of essays on the cosmos and on how a state ought to be run. He lived from about 179 to 104 BCE, though scholars think the book was added to and rearranged for a long time after his death. The passage that matters here is chapter 57, titled in English *Things of the Same Category Move Each Other*.

It makes the case plainly, and it does not begin with omens. It begins with things anyone can watch. Water runs toward whatever is already damp. Fire turns toward whatever is already dry. Things avoid what is unlike them and move toward what matches. Good things summon others of their kind, the text says, and bad things summon others of their kind, and things of a kind respond to one another and rise together.

*Two Lutes*

Then it reaches for an instrument, and this is the image Dong is most famous for.

Take two lutes, the *qin* and the *se*, and tune them. Set them where they can answer each other. Strike the *gong* note on one, and the *gong* string on the other sounds though nobody touched it. Strike the *shang* note, and the matching *shang* answers. The five tones work the same way. Like calls like across open air. The book is careful about what this is not. It is not a god plucking the second lute. It is not magic. The numbers of the notes match, so the strings respond. People see no hand move and call it chance, or say the lute sounded by itself. Dong refuses that. Something made it sound. The cause is real. You just cannot see it. The same pattern, he says, runs through the rest of life. Good summons good. Bad summons bad. A horse answers a horse. An ox answers an ox. What matches meets.

And then comes the turn that matters here. A person's own mood belongs to the same order. Worry looks for rest. Joy looks for movement. What is in a man answers is what is in the world because they are the same kind of thing.

Dong did not only write about this. He put it into practice.

*Summer Drought*

When Dong Zhongshu was appointed chancellor to the king of Jiangdu, around 125 BCE, he walked straight into an environmental and political crisis. Jiangdu had been scorched by drought for months. The crops were dying. The rivers were mudflats. Local officials were growing desperate.

Before Dong arrived, the magistrates tried to solve the crisis by doubling down on heat, force, and violence. They believed malicious spirits were behind the drought, and they decided to wage war against the sky. They built an open-air altar in the blazing sun, bound a witch, and set her ablaze on the pyre. Their logic was rooted in desperation. Their execution violated the principle Dong taught. They added roaring fire, which is yang, human killing, which is yang, and midday heat, which is yang, to an environment already choking on dry, aggressive yang. The result was disastrous. The scorching heat radiated out from the altar and set nearby withered crops on fire. The air grew even drier. The sky stayed blindingly clear. The town was worse off than before.

When Dong arrived, he halted the violent rituals at once. In the *Chunqiu fanlu* he lays out the foundational mistake. You cannot attract cool water by breeding hot fire. Like calls unto like. The universe answers what matches it. A severe drought is an overwhelming surge of yang. To force heaven to respond, you do not throw more yang into the air. You cultivate a dense pocket of yin, coldness, darkness, water, and female energy, so that the atmosphere has something of the same kind to answer.

Dong issued strict instructions and turned the whole district into a focal point of yin. He ordered the southern city gates, the direction of heat and yang, shut. He had the northern city gates, the direction of cold and yin, flung wide open. All martial exercises, blacksmithing, and heavy male field labor were banned. Men were told to remain indoors to suppress the town's aggressive energy. Women, who embodied yin, were brought to the forefront. They were directed to gather along the northern riverbanks, sing low songs, and spill cold well water back into the earth. Villagers collected frogs and subterranean spring water and placed them in shallow pools along the northern boundary.

According to Han records of Dong's tenure in Jiangdu, the shift was palpable. Within hours of the city settling into that quiet, cold yin alignment, dark rain clouds gathered over the northern gates. The storm broke and brought heavy, cool rain over Jiangdu, and the drought broke. For Dong, the sudden downpour was not a divine reward for good behavior. It was the mechanical outcome of *ganying*. When you build a vessel of yin large enough, the yin in the sky answers.

He was not selling a method for getting what he wanted. He was describing a universe in which nothing is sealed off from anything else.

## The old book and its readers

The *I Ching*, the Book of Changes, was already ancient when Dong Zhongshu was alive. It is a book of sixty-four figures called hexagrams that are used for divination and read for wisdom  For centuries Chinese scholars kept writing commentary on it, layer over layer, trying to say what the old figures meant. Ten of those commentaries came to be treated as part of the classic itself, and they are known as the Ten Wings. One of them is the *Wenyan*, the Commentary on the Words. Tradition liked to credit it to Confucius. Scholars generally date it to the Warring States period and the early Han, and assume many hands.

The passage that matters comes from the Wenyan. It comments on the first hexagram, Qian, often called the Creative, and on the line about a dragon in flight.  The commentators are trying to explain something they had noticed. Why does the world turn toward a certain kind of person? Their answer is simple. Things of the same sound respond to each other. Things of the same breath seek each other.

 Richard Wilhelm, whose translation carried the *I Ching* into English and German, rendered the pair as things that accord in tone vibrating together, and things with an affinity in their inmost natures seeking one another out.

Then, like Dong Zhongshu, they go to what anyone can see. Water flows toward what is wet. Fire turns toward what is dry. Clouds gather where the dragon is. Wind rises where the tiger walks. When a truly good man appears, they say, everything alive turns to look at him. Each thing follows its kind.

That phrase, things of the same breath seek each other, is as close as the Chinese classics come to the modern sentence about energy attracting similar energy. And notice what the commentators do with it. They do not use it to promise anyone a result. They use it to explain influence. What a man is draws a matching answer out of the people around him, and he does not have to ask for it.

## A circle of men in Spain

Jump forward more than a thousand years, to thirteenth-century Spain, and to a book that pretends to be much older than it is.

The *Zohar*, meaning Splendor, is the central text of Kabbalah, the mystical reading of the Jewish scriptures. It is written in Aramaic and framed as the conversation of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and his students, who lived in the second century. Most scholars think it was actually composed and published in thirteenth-century Spain, in the circle around a man named Moses de León, who put his own teaching in the mouths of the ancient dead. That is worth knowing, because it means the book is medieval, not ancient, and it should not be presented as older than it is.

What it says, though, is closer to the concern of this chapter than anything else in the tradition. The *Zohar* describes God through a structure of emanations called the sefirot, and it teaches that nothing comes down from above until something has been raised from below. The Aramaic phrase is *it'aruta di-letata*, arousal from below, answered by *it'aruta di-le'eila*, arousal from above. In everything, one passage says, something must first be stirred up down here, by an act or by a word, or by displaying some likeness to the thing being sought, and then the answer comes from above. The lines quoted here are from Daniel Matt's translation, the Pritzker Edition, which is the standard English text.

Then it gets specific, and it gets emotional. What is shown below determines what is given. If the divine presence shows a radiant face from below, the book says, she is lit up from above, and if she shows sadness, Judgment comes to meet her. A person's joy, another line says, draws down a joy greater than itself. The mystics hung the whole idea on a single line from Psalm 100, serve the LORD with gladness, and read it to mean that gladness is not decoration on worship. Gladness is the thing that reaches.

Set that beside the camp at Kadesh and the shape is the same, running the other way. Sorrow rehearsed below is met by Judgment. Joy held below draws joy toward it. The tone a person carries is treated as an opening, and something answers through it.

One caution belongs here, and it belongs to the whole chapter. A principle that says inner state meets its match can be turned into a weapon against people who are suffering, and it has been. The *Zohar* is not handing anyone a rule for deciding that a grieving man brought his grief on himself. Neither is Numbers. The book of Job is forty-two chapters of that argument being made by Job's friends and rejected, and at the end of it God tells the friends they were the ones who spoke wrongly. Keep the principle and refuse the cruelty. The two do not come as a set.

## Back to Scripture

Three traditions, no contact between them, each convinced that what is held inside goes out looking for its own kind. Which brings us back to the book this one is mostly about, where the same observation appears earlier, more often, and more bluntly than in any of them.

Israel put a good deal of its thinking into short lines meant to be remembered, and one of those lines carries this whole chapter inside it. It is Proverbs 10:24, sitting in the long stretch of contrast sayings that opens the collection headed the proverbs of Solomon. What the wicked dreads will come upon him, but the desire of the righteous will be granted.

Look at how that is built. One half is about dread. The Hebrew word is *pachad*, the fear a person broods on and keeps expecting, and the verse says that thing overtakes him. The other half is about desire, *taavah*, the thing a man leans toward and orients a life around, and the verse says that thing is granted. Two inner postures, two matching outcomes, one sentence. Law of Attraction teaching would call both halves attraction. Proverbs is not making a promise with a delivery date. It is doing what wisdom literature does, which is to report a pattern that shows up often enough to be worth telling your children about.

The clearest statement of it comes from Jesus, on a road, to two men who could not see. The scene is Matthew 9:27 through 31, tucked in among a run of healings.

They had been following him and shouting, Son of David, have mercy on us. When he finally turned around he did not ask them what they wanted, which he could presumably guess. He asked them a question about their inner state. Do you believe that I am able to do this? They said yes. He touched their eyes and said seven words that could serve as the summary of everything in this chapter. According to your faith be it done to you. The Greek word behind faith is *pistis*, which means trust that someone is able to act, not a general sunniness about life.

Not according to your effort. Not according to your worthiness, or your paperwork, or how loudly you shouted on the road. According to your faith. What was in them set what came to them. That is manifestation under another name.

And that is the answer to the night at Kadesh. Nothing unusual happened out there. The same principle was running, in the direction nobody wants it to run. A whole nation practiced defeat out loud until defeat was the road in front of them. They were not cursed for a bad mood. They were answered, in their own words, by a God who had been listening.

## Who is doing the answering

So the parallel is real, and it is not a small one. Everyone in this chapter noticed the same thing. Dong Zhongshu noticed it, the commentators on the *I Ching* noticed it, the mystics in Spain noticed it, and Israel had it written down before any of them. What is inside is not sealed off. It meets its match.

Where they part is over what is on the other end of it.

Modern Law of Attraction teaching usually answers that question with energy. Frequency, vibration, a field that returns what you broadcast, working the way electricity works, indifferent to who is using it. The Chinese texts answer with resonance in a living cosmos, an order running from heaven down through the ruler to the crops. The *Zohar* answers with a heaven that leans down when something is raised toward it. All three, in their own way, describe a mechanism.

Scripture answers with a person. At Kadesh nobody says the wilderness resonated with their fear. What Numbers 14:28 says is, what you have said in my hearing, I will do to you. First person. Someone was in the room. And that changes the character of the whole thing, because a mechanism can be operated and a person has to be dealt with. It also means the seed is never neutral. Faith, dread, mercy, and contempt are not settings on a dial. They are ways of standing toward God and toward the people around you, and Scripture is interested in which one you are practicing, not only in what it produces.

## Not only what you feel

One more widening before this chapter closes, because the Bible does not confine the principle to the inside of a person's head.

Paul says it about deeds, in Galatians 6:7 and 8. Do not be deceived, he writes; a man reaps what he sows. He is not talking about wishing, and the next line keeps him honest, because what he contrasts is sowing to the flesh and sowing to the Spirit, with corruption on one side and life on the other. The seed has moral content. But the mechanism is the one we have been following all along. Kind returns kind.

Jesus says it about mercy, in Luke 6:37 and 38, inside a block of teaching about loving enemies and lending without expecting return. Do not judge, and you will not be judged. Forgive, and you will be forgiven. Give, and it will be given to you. The measure you use is the measure you meet.

And Scripture likes to show it before it explains it. In Genesis 27, Jacob deceives his blind father to steal a blessing, and by Genesis 29 he is living under a father-in-law who deceives him at every turn, and the narrator never once stops to point it out. Psalm 7:14 through 16 says a man digs a pit and falls into it, and that the trouble he made for someone else comes back down on his own head. Nobody in those passages is manifesting anything. They are simply living in a world where what you set in motion has a way of finding you.

## The next question

Put all of it together and the principle stands, in three traditions and in Scripture most of all. What is inside goes out and comes back wearing the same face. Dread overtakes. Desire is granted. Mercy meets mercy. A camp full of fear talks its way into forty years of wilderness. Faith on a dusty road opens two pairs of eyes.

Which leaves one thing unaccounted for. If what is inside is what comes back, then everything depends on what is in there, and on how it got there. That is where this goes next.
