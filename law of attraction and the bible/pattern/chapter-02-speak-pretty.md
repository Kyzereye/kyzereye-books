## Chapter argument in one paragraph

Modern Law of Attraction teaching often says that a dominant inner state draws matching circumstances. The older texts in this chapter do not state that modern formula. They do, however, preserve related patterns. Hermetic texts describe correspondence and likeness. Buddhist texts connect the moral quality of mind, speech, and action with a corresponding result. The Upanishads connect desire, action, becoming, and rebirth. The Bible repeatedly describes conduct returning as harvest, measure, consequence, or poetic justice under God. The honest comparison is **family resemblance**, not identity.

**Short form:** LoA speaks of attraction. These older texts more often speak of correspondence, karma, harvest, judgment, and becoming.

---

## Guardrail: what “like attracts like” means here

The chapter uses “like attracts like” as a modern heading for several older return-in-kind patterns. That heading must not be pushed backward into the texts as though their authors used LoA vocabulary.

- **LoA attraction:** inner state is said to draw matching circumstances.
- **Hermetic correspondence:** levels or realities resemble one another; like can know like.
- **Buddhist karma:** intentional action shaped by wholesome or unwholesome mind bears corresponding fruit when conditions mature.
- **Upanishadic karma and becoming:** desire leads toward resolve and action, and action conditions what one becomes or where one is reborn.
- **Biblical sowing and reaping:** conduct has fitting consequences within God’s moral and relational order.

These are related structures, not interchangeable doctrines.

## Witness 1 — Hermetic texts

### A. Emerald Tablet

**Text used in Chapter 2**

Isaac Newton’s English translation reads:

> “That which is below is like that which is above and that which is above is like that which is below to do the miracle of one only thing.”

**Location and history**

- The earliest recoverable forms are Arabic, preserved in the *Book of the Secret of Creation and the Craft of Nature*, usually dated to approximately the late eighth or ninth century CE.
- Latin translations circulated from roughly the twelfth century onward.
- A lost Greek or Syriac source is possible, but no earlier manuscript has been recovered.
- Therefore, call the Emerald Tablet a **medieval Hermetic/alchemical text with roots claimed in an older Hermetic tradition**, not securely an ancient document.

**What the line safely supports**

- Correspondence between above and below.
- A unified reality in which different levels resemble or answer to one another.
- A bridge between Chapter 2’s matching and Chapter 3’s inner/outer relationship.

**What it does not directly support**

- It does not say that thoughts magnetically attract desired events.
- “Above” and “below” do not simply mean “inner mind” and “outer circumstances.”
- The popular slogan “as above, so below” is a shortened later form; quote an identified translation rather than treating the slogan as the entire text.

### B. *Corpus Hermeticum* XI.20

The passage is in *Corpus Hermeticum* XI.20, in a discourse in which Mind teaches Hermes how the divine can be apprehended. A common English rendering is:

> “If then you do not make yourself equal to God, you cannot apprehend God; for like is known by like.”

**Preferred scholarly edition**

- Brian P. Copenhaver, *Hermetica: The Greek Corpus Hermeticum and the Latin Asclepius in a New English Translation, with Notes and Introduction* (Cambridge University Press, 1992), *Corpus Hermeticum* XI.20.
- Check Copenhaver’s exact wording before publication. The familiar wording above is often associated with Walter Scott or later quotation collections, and should not be labeled as Copenhaver without verification.

**What the passage safely supports**

- Knowledge by likeness: the knower must become capable of apprehending what is known.
- Transformation of the knower, expansion of thought, and participation in divine reality.

**What it does not directly support**

- This is primarily an epistemological and spiritual statement — **like knows like** — not a claim that like circumstances are attracted.
- It is not the same teaching as the Emerald Tablet. They share likeness language but perform different jobs.

**Best way to say it aloud**

“The Emerald Tablet describes correspondence between levels of reality. *Corpus Hermeticum* XI.20 describes knowledge through likeness. Both use matching, but neither presents the modern Law of Attraction formula.”

**Do not use as ancient evidence**

*The Kybalion* was published in 1908. It is important to modern New Thought and modern Hermetic reception, but it is not an ancient Hermetic source.

## Witness 2 — Buddhism

### *Dhammapada* 1–2

**Text used in Chapter 2**

The current draft uses Acharya Buddharakkhita’s translation:

> “Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.”

> “Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with a pure mind a person speaks or acts happiness follows him like his never-departing shadow.”

**Source**

- *Dhammapada*, Yamakavagga (“Pairs”), verses 1–2.
- Acharya Buddharakkhita, *The Dhammapada: The Buddha’s Path of Wisdom* (Buddhist Publication Society, 1985).
- Online text: Buddhist Publication Society / Access to Insight.

**Translation caution**

Buddharakkhita’s wording is not automatically free for unrestricted commercial quotation. The Access to Insight notice allows specified noncommercial/free redistribution and retains other rights. Before publication, either obtain permission, keep the quotation within fair-use limits with attribution, or select a verified public-domain translation.

**Immediate context**

- The chapter title “Pairs” matters: impure/pure mind, suffering/happiness, wheel/shadow.
- The verses join **mind → speech/action → consequence**.
- In Buddhist explanation, karma is intentional or volitional action, not merely any event and not fate.
- Karma may bear fruit when supporting conditions are present; it is not necessarily instant or one-to-one.

**What the verses safely support in Chapter 2**

- The moral quality of mind expressed in speech and action is followed by a corresponding quality of result.
- The wheel and shadow are vivid return images.
- Inner state matters because it gives moral character to action.

**What to save for Chapter 3**

- “Mind precedes” and “mind-wrought” as the major inner-first argument.
- The larger Buddhist discipline of training and directing the mind.

**What not to claim**

- Buddhism does not teach that every circumstance is personally attracted.
- Karma is not “positive thoughts bring prizes.”
- These verses do not justify telling sufferers that they created every misfortune.
- Buddhism does not posit a permanent creative self in the same way some LoA systems do.

**Best way to say it aloud**

“In Chapter 2 I stress the images of what follows — suffering like a wheel, happiness like a shadow. In Chapter 3 I return to the first half of the verses, where mind is called the forerunner.”

## Witness 3 — Hindu Upanishads

### A. *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* 4.4.5–7

**Text used in Chapter 2**

Max Müller’s public-domain translation includes:

> “A man of good acts will become good, a man of bad acts, bad. He becomes pure by pure deeds, bad by bad deeds.”

> “And as is his desire, so is his will; and as is his will, so is his deed; and whatever deed he does, that he will reap.”

**Why the larger unit matters**

- Verse 4.4.5 connects character, desire, resolve, deed, and result.
- Verse 4.4.6 continues into attachment and rebirth: the mind goes toward the object to which it is attached, accompanied by its deed.
- Verse 4.4.7 turns toward freedom from desire and realization of Brahman.
- The destination of the teaching is liberation from desire-driven rebirth, not improved acquisition within it.

**What it safely supports**

- Desire is formative.
- Desire moves toward resolve; resolve moves toward deed; deed bears a result.
- A person becomes good through good action and bad through bad action.

**What not to claim**

- The passage does not say desire alone instantly manifests an object.
- “Reap” belongs to karma and rebirth in this context.
- The Upanishad’s final aim is release from the desire/action cycle, which differs sharply from desire-centered LoA practice.

### B. *Mundaka Upanishad* 3.2.1–2

Max Müller’s public-domain translation reads:

> “He who forms desires in his mind, is born again through his desires here and there. But to him whose desires are fulfilled and who is conscious of the true Self (within himself) all desires vanish, even here on earth.”

Another translation renders the first half as:

> “He, who broods on and longs for objects of desire, is born there and there with such desires.”

**Immediate context**

- The contrast is between continued rebirth through desire and liberation through knowledge of the Self.
- “Desires fulfilled” in the second half does not simply mean obtaining every desired object. It points toward completion, contentment, or the ending of desire in Self-realization.

**What it safely supports**

- Cherished desire directs future becoming and rebirth.
- A person is born into conditions corresponding to desire in the text’s karmic framework.

**What not to claim**

- This is not permission to use desire as an acquisition technique.
- The verse ultimately recommends transcendence of binding desire.

**Best way to say the two Upanishads aloud**

“The *Brihadaranyaka* traces desire into resolve, deed, result, and becoming. The *Mundaka* says desire continues into rebirth. Both give desire real formative power, but both place that power inside a larger teaching whose goal is freedom from binding desire.”

## Three witnesses — accurate short comparison

- **Hermetic:** likeness and correspondence make matching intelligible, but do not directly state attraction.
- **Buddhist:** intention gives action its moral character, and action bears corresponding fruit.
- **Upanishadic:** desire conditions resolve, action, becoming, and rebirth, while liberation ends bondage to desire.

These passages establish that matching and return are old patterns. They do not establish that all ancient traditions secretly taught one identical LoA system.

## Bible chain (main development — know these cold)

### 1. Galatians 6:7–10 — anchor

**Read:** Galatians 5:13–26 and 6:1–10, not only 6:7.

**Context**

- “Flesh” and “Spirit” continue Paul’s contrast from chapter 5.
- Sowing to the Spirit is expressed through restoring others, carrying burdens, sharing, persevering in doing good, and serving the community.
- The stated harvest is corruption versus eternal life. The horizon is partly eschatological, not merely an immediate lifestyle result.
- Verse 9 adds time and perseverance: harvest comes “in due season,” not on demand.

**Safe claim**

Paul states an unmistakable kind-of-seed/kind-of-harvest principle within Christian moral life under God.

**Avoid claim**

Paul is not describing vibrational attraction, promising a chosen object, or saying every thought quickly becomes an event.

**Best phrasing**

“Paul’s return-law is moral, communal, spiritual, and finally eschatological. It still teaches that the kind of life sown determines the kind of harvest reaped.”

### 2. Luke 6:27–38 — measure for measure

**Read:** Luke 6:20–49, especially 6:27–38.

**Context**

- The controlling themes are enemy-love, non-retaliation, generosity, forgiveness, and mercy.
- Verse 36 grounds the behavior in God’s character: “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.”
- Judging, condemning, forgiving/releasing, and giving form a chain of matching responses.
- The overflowing measure likely evokes grain pressed and shaken into a container, then poured into a person’s lap or garment.

**Safe claim**

Jesus uses explicit reciprocity: the measure one uses becomes the measure returned.

**Important tension**

The passage also tells followers to break ordinary human reciprocity by loving enemies without demanding return. The matching result is placed within God’s merciful economy, not a transaction controlled by the giver.

**Avoid claim**

Luke 6:38 is not primarily a fundraising promise or a guaranteed financial multiplication formula.

### 3. 2 Corinthians 8–9, especially 9:6–11 — generous sowing

**Context**

- Paul is organizing a collection from Gentile churches for economically distressed believers in Jerusalem.
- The gift meets material need, expresses equality and fellowship, and produces thanksgiving to God.
- God supplies and multiplies “seed” so that the giver can continue in generosity and “every good work.”
- Verse 10 identifies the harvest as “the harvest of your righteousness.”

**Safe claim**

Generous sowing leads to generous harvest, and God supplies enough for generosity to continue.

**Avoid claim**

The text does not promise that donations will make the donor personally wealthy. Enrichment in verse 11 is directed “for all generosity.”

### 4. Proverbs 11:24–25 — generosity and withholding

**Textual notes**

- One “scatters” or gives freely and nevertheless increases.
- The contrasting person withholds what is right or justly due and comes to want.
- “The generous person will be enriched” may be rendered literally as the “soul of blessing” being “made fat,” an ancient image of abundance and well-being.
- “The one who waters will himself be watered” is the clearest return-in-kind line.

**Safe claim**

Wisdom observes the paradox that generosity can open abundance while unjust withholding can produce lack.

**Avoid claim**

Proverbs are wisdom observations, not mechanical guarantees for every individual case or timetable.

### 5. Proverbs 22:8 — injustice as seed

**Text**

> “Whoever sows injustice will reap calamity” (wording varies by translation).

**Safe claim**

This is one of the Bible’s clearest moral return-in-kind formulations: the seed is injustice and the harvest is trouble or calamity.

**Avoid claim**

Do not detach it from ethics and turn it into a neutral law of frequency.

### 6. Genesis 27 and 29:15–30 — the deceiver deceived

**Why the connection is strong**

- Jacob takes the elder brother’s place; later Leah, the elder sister, is substituted for Rachel.
- Isaac is deceived in blindness/darkness; Jacob discovers the deception only when morning comes.
- In Genesis 29:25, Jacob asks why Laban “deceived” him. The Hebrew root echoes Genesis 27:35, where Isaac says Jacob came “deceitfully.”
- Laban’s appeal to the firstborn’s priority exposes the irony of Jacob’s treatment of Esau.

**Safe claim**

The narrative is deliberately shaped as poetic justice: the deceiver experiences deception of a closely matching kind.

**Avoid claim**

The narrator does not stop to announce a universal metaphysical law, and God does not explicitly say, “I caused this because of Genesis 27.” Present it as strong literary symmetry, not a quoted doctrine.

### 7. Psalm 7:14–16 — planned harm returns

**Context**

- The wicked conceives trouble, gives birth to falsehood, digs a pit, and falls into it.
- Verse 16 removes ambiguity: trouble returns on the offender’s own head, and violence descends on the offender’s skull.
- The pit is probably hunting/trapping imagery.

**Safe claim**

This is explicit poetic reversal: intended harm recoils upon its author.

**Avoid claim**

The psalm frames this under divine justice, not impersonal attraction.

### 8. Numbers 13:31–33; 14:1–4, 20–35 — the dark example

**Context**

- The spies report formidable enemies; the congregation responds with fear, complaint, and a wish that they had died in Egypt or the wilderness.
- The decisive issue is not speech by itself. It is sustained unbelief, rebellion, rejection of the promised land, and refusal to trust God despite prior signs.
- In 14:28 God says he will do what he heard them say. The immediate reference is their wish in 14:2 to die in the wilderness.
- Their children, whom they predicted would become prey, are instead brought into the land. The judgment is exact and ironic.

**Safe claim**

Their spoken wish becomes the form of their judgment because their words express a settled posture of unbelief and refusal.

**Avoid claim**

Do not say fear magically manifested giants, defeat, or death. God pronounces judgment in response to covenant rebellion. This is the sharpest place where biblical agency differs from an impersonal LoA mechanism.

## Where parallel holds / where they part

**Holds**
- Inner disposition becomes speech, conduct, habit, and character.
- Conduct has consequences and often returns in a fitting form.
- Repeated desire, attention, and action shape what a person becomes.
- The quality of the seed matters more than a passing wish.

**Parts**
- **LoA:** often vibrational matching aimed at desired circumstances.
- **Bible:** moral, relational, communal, and eschatological return under a personal God.
- **Buddhism:** volitional action and karmic fruit without a permanent creator-self.
- **Upanishads:** desire, karma, becoming, rebirth, and eventual liberation from binding desire.
- **Hermetic texts:** correspondence, likeness, spiritual knowledge, and participation in divine reality.

**Do not flatten the differences:** similar structure does not prove identical metaphysics.
